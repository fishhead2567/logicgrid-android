Logic grid puzzles — the kind with a short introduction, a numbered list of
clues, and a chart you fill in with circles and crosses until only one answer
survives.

Fifty puzzles ship inside the app. A fresh install with no network at all has
every one of them.

<b>How it works</b>

Tap a cell, mark it O or X. Circling a cell rules out the rest of its row and
column automatically, because that is pencil-work the rules force rather than
a deduction you should have to repeat by hand.

Anything that <i>is</i> a deduction stays yours unless you ask for help.
Transitivity and last-cell-standing live behind an opt-in Assist setting, so
the app never quietly solves the part you were working on.

The answer sheet reads off whatever you have actually determined, not whatever
blocks you happened to fill in. Pin two of the three blocks on a three-category
grid and the third follows; you do not have to write it out to be told you are
done.

<b>Check my work</b>

Tells you whether what you have can still be right. Not how many marks are
wrong — a player told "one" knows it was recent — and not which, because that
hands over part of the answer. Just whether you have gone wrong somewhere, the
way glancing at the back of a puzzle magazine does.

<b>Every puzzle is proved before it ships</b>

Each one has been verified to have exactly one solution, reachable by
deduction without guessing unless its difficulty says otherwise. The generator
that makes them is free software too, and the puzzles are a separate repository
of plain JSON under the same licence.

<b>What it does not do</b>

No account. No sign-in. No analytics, no advertising, no tracking of any kind.
Your progress is stored on the device and goes nowhere else.

The only network request the app ever makes is an unauthenticated fetch of
public puzzle files, to pick up new puzzles when there are some. It runs in the
background, it is never on the path to playing, and every failure is silent —
the app has a full set either way. If an update would replace puzzles you had
started, it says so first, and your solved count survives even when the grids
cannot.

<b>Fits the screen it is on</b>

The chart pans and zooms with sticky headers on both axes, labels can be shown
upright or turned a quarter turn as a printed chart has them, and the whole app
follows the system's light or dark setting.
